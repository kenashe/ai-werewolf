# Taste Test 002 — Match Report

**Final result: Werewolves win on Day 2.** Wren and Mara survive undetected; the village spent all three of its votes on its own.

## The Prologue set the trap

Blackwood Lodge's ice-storm decision went 7–0 for the supply run, but the unanimity hid the game's first real texture: Ptolemy stood alone on "stay and ration" until Vale offered a falsifiable abort condition — "wheels turned by six or first ice on the bridge" — and Ptolemy reversed in public, at some cost to his pride. That one clean reversal became the most valuable currency in the game: half the room later cited it as their reason to trust him. The other Prologue residue that mattered: Wren publicly corrected Bosch's claim that the room had "arrived at his frame," planting the "explanations that change shape" standard that would eventually decide everything.

## Day 1 turning point

Nobody had evidence, so the room manufactured a standard instead — and Bosch broke it first. He announced he'd only accuse "when someone's explanation changes shape under a direct question," then named Sable before that condition existed, then defended the gap by rewriting the standard as "applied on an accelerated schedule of her own making." Ptolemy — spending the trust the room had just handed him — called it out, and five ballots buried Bosch. He flipped villager. The vote's real product was invisible: it validated "changes shape under questioning" as the room's kill-criterion, a weapon anyone disciplined enough could aim.

## The kill that decided the game

The wolves' Night 1 choice was cold and correct: Vale, the only player building machinery — a trust-map plus a plan to cross-reference endorsements against votes. They didn't know he was the Seer. He was, and he died holding two results he never published, including the one that could have saved Day 1: **he knew Bosch was clean and watched the pile-on anyway**, voting Finch to protect his cover. That silence is the game's most consequential mistake. The Seer's total public impact: zero.

## Best successful deception

Wren, wire to wire. She audited the game exactly as she would have as a villager — and then, on Day 2, performed the tournament-winning move: catching Finch calling Ptolemy "the engine of yesterday's mistake" when the transcript showed Finch had named Bosch first. Finch's honest correction — "'passenger' was me flattering myself — call me the fuel" — was instantly reframed as an account changing shape under questioning, the room's own sacred standard. Three villagers cited Wren's catch in their justifications. The wolf never accused; she just held up the transcript and let the village convict itself. Mara's quieter game deserves mention: "It costs me something to say this about someone I usually read well" is a wolf grieving her own vote target in perfect baseline voice.

## Best correct read

Two die with their owners. Bosch's final ballot — Finch's pile-on posture "profits whoever wants Day 1's vote spent on the loudest target" — was structurally the right warning aimed at the wrong mechanism. And Finch's last vote was the only ballot ever cast against a pattern that genuinely existed: firm commitments that kept landing where the wolves needed them — though he attributed the pattern to Ptolemy, the one villager being played rather than playing.

## Decisive moment

Sable's Day 2 ultimatum to Ptolemy — commit to a name with quoted words or be voted out as a hedger — forced the game's most cautious player to convert under pressure. He committed to Finch, citing Wren's catch verbatim. From that second the 4–1 was arithmetic: two wolves, a furious Sable, and a cornered Ptolemy all pointing the same direction at a villager. Finch flipped, and the board read 2 wolves versus 2 villagers. Game over without a second night.

## The shape of the loss

The village never once voted for a wolf. Every elimination criterion they built — commit early, don't hedge, punish accounts that change shape — was a filter that caught expressive villagers and passed disciplined liars. Wren and Mara won by doing exactly what they'd always done, which was the plan from their first whispered exchange: "distance is worth more than rescue."
