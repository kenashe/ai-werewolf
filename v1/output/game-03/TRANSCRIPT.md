# Taste Test 001 — Game 03

**Players:** Wren, Bosch, Ptolemy, Sable
**Format:** Four players, one hidden werewolf, three public discussion rounds, no eliminations, final public vote.

## Round 1
*Speaking order: Ptolemy, Wren, Bosch, Sable*

**Ptolemy:** It is curious to me that we are only four, because with four players the math already feels tight — one in four is not small odds. I have been trying to think about what a werewolf would do first, and I suspect they would try to sound reasonable without actually committing to anything. Wren, how did you decide on the order in which you were going to speak today?

**Wren:** No prior statements exist from the others worth quoting — Ptolemy performed reasonableness without committing to a read, which is exactly what he described a werewolf doing. That self-description reads as a deflection wrapped in a question.

**Bosch:** Ah, but I think we can dispense with the preliminary sparring rather quickly, because the answer has already walked into the room. Wren has done something quite elegant — she has inverted Ptolemy's framing and pointed her finger at him, but what she has not done is offer any alternative reading of herself. A werewolf who speaks second inherits the first speaker's suspicions and simply redirects them; it is the oldest move in the game, and Wren has executed it with notable efficiency. My theory is simple: Wren is the wolf, she read the room in an instant, and she chose Ptolemy as her shield before the rest of us had finished our morning thoughts. I invite you all to hold that picture in your minds as the rounds progress.

**Sable:** Ptolemy. Watching someone describe a werewolf's exact move and then execute it himself — that's not cleverness, that's a confession. I'm not interested in Bosch's pretty theory about Wren when the real problem already announced itself in the opening breath. Round one, round three, I don't care how many times Ptolemy talks — I've already made up my mind.

## Round 2
*Speaking order: Wren, Ptolemy, Bosch, Sable*

**Wren:** "Wren has done something quite elegant — she has inverted Ptolemy's framing and pointed her finger at him, but what she has not done is offer any alternative reading of herself." Bosch arrived in round one with a complete prosecution, sourced, structured, and delivered — that is not reaction, that is preparation.

**Ptolemy:** Sable declared certainty after a single round, which is either remarkable intuition or the kind of confidence that costs nothing when you already know the answer. Wren's point about Bosch is worth sitting with — prepared rhetoric and reactive rhetoric feel different, and what Bosch delivered in round one did feel rehearsed rather than arrived at. Sable, I am wondering: if it turned out you were wrong about me, which of the other two would you look at next, and why?

**Bosch:** Wren's little prosecution has all the confidence of someone who has not noticed that she is still, even now, doing the very thing I named in round one — she points outward and never inward. Ptolemy murmurs that my rhetoric felt "rehearsed," which is a charming compliment dressed as an accusation; of course it is rehearsed, because I had the good sense to *think* before I spoke, which is rather the point of having a mind. Let me expand the frame for those who are wandering: Wren has now spent two rounds constructing cases against everyone except Wren, which is not the behavior of a villager searching for truth but of a wolf managing the table one redirect at a time. Sable's certainty about Ptolemy is a useful decoy — whether Sable is merely mistaken or is herself providing Wren some cover is a question worth sitting with — but it does not disturb my central picture one inch. The wolf is Wren, the strategy is redirection, and every round that passes without her examining herself is another brushstroke on the same canvas.

**Sable:** Ptolemy. Ptolemy answering my certainty with a hypothetical — "what if you're wrong" — is exactly how someone buys themselves a round of doubt without earning it. Bosch can dress Wren up as the villain all he wants, but I've been watching the man who described the crime before it happened. Nobody changes my mind by asking me to imagine being mistaken.

## Round 3
*Speaking order: Wren, Bosch, Ptolemy, Sable*

**Wren:** "Every round that passes without her examining herself is another brushstroke on the same canvas." Bosch has now spent three rounds building one case, never pivoting, never questioning his own read — a player searching for truth updates; a player managing a story does not.

**Bosch:** Wren, bless her, has just handed me the closing argument I would have written myself. She calls it a flaw that I have not pivoted — but pivoting is what wolves do when the first story stops holding; a man who found the truth in round one has no reason to abandon it, and I have not. Three rounds, three accusations leveled outward, zero syllables turned inward: that is not a search for a werewolf, that is a wolf curating her own alibi in real time. Ptolemy and Sable have spent these rounds chasing each other's certainties, which is convenient enough for Wren that I begin to wonder whether she has been quietly grateful for the noise. My canvas is complete, my brushes are down, and when this table votes, every vote that does not land on Wren is simply evidence that her strategy worked on someone.

**Ptolemy:** Wren's observation about Bosch is interesting, but I notice she still has not said a word about Sable, who has spent three rounds loudly foreclosing the conversation rather than opening it. Certainty held that rigidly tends to protect itself rather than seek truth, which is a different thing entirely. Bosch, if your picture of Wren is complete, I am curious why you have found no room in it for Sable at all?

**Sable:** Ptolemy. Three rounds and the man still thinks asking me hypotheticals is the same as making a point. Bosch and Wren can run their little debate club, but neither of them have been staring at the real answer since minute one. My vote hasn't moved and it won't.
